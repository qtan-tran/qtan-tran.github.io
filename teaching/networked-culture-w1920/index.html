Teaching

Seminar · Winter 2019–20 · 56-107 (5 LP) · ESA W, Room 209

Networked culture: Information, network society and new forms of knowledge

University of Hamburg

Course description

This course aims to introduce students to the main themes of information and network society and understanding of the basic theoretical approaches and concepts of networked culture. We explore how the development of digital information infrastructure has paved the way for new types of social methods and forms of knowledge. For example, what is sharing and how is sharing mediated? The bridge to a networked culture entails the necessary technologies to promote linkages, collaboration, and more democratic dialogue between institutions and the public.

In the theoretical lens, we examine “networked public sphere” and online community as being able to convey both civic and social ideas, which would contribute to building a sense of interest, belonging, and collaboration. On the practical level, we examine a set of digital practices such as open sourcing, social tagging, online sharing, and cases in the GLAM sectors to illustrate different senses of community and common institutional missions such as user engagement, openness, and social inclusion.

There was no class in December. Double sessions took place on 14, 21 and 28 November, 10 am–2 pm.

Main literature

  • Castells, M. (2010). The rise of the network society (2nd ed., with a new preface). Wiley-Blackwell.
  • Van Dijck, J., Poell, T., & Waal, M. de. (2018). The platform society: Public values in a connective world. Oxford University Press.
  • Van Dijk, J. J. A. (2006). The network society: Social aspects of new media (2nd ed.). SAGE.

Teaching schedule

Session 1 (17 October 2019): Introduction to the course

  • Course’s structure, objectives and learning outcomes
  • Course components and assessment types
  • Some background concepts

Session 2 (24 October 2019): Networked culture

  • Manuel Castells’ view: a network society, a network-based social structure
  • Organisation and relation of elements/units in a network
  • Understand the typology of networks and types of network communication

Reading: Castells (2010, pp. 500–509); Van Dijk (2006, Chapter 2).

Session 3 (31 October 2019): Public holiday

Session 4 (7 November 2019): Networks and social worlds

  • Does the network society fray social ties or thicken our pre-existing relations? / The two-sided arguments
  • How networks exploit digital technologies to break down socio-cultural barriers?

Reading: Benkler (2006, Chapter 10); Castells (2015, pp. 246–271).

Sessions 5+6 (14 November 2019): Study platform

  • What is datafication? How can platforms steer user interaction through the selection or curation of data?
  • Discuss how platforms reconstruct relations of authority, and establish new forms of knowledge and new ways of interpretation

Reading: Van Dijck, Poell & Waal (2018, Chapter 1); Gillespie (2017). Optional: Van Dijck, Poell & Waal (2018, Chapter 2).

Sessions 7+8 (21 November 2019): Study infrastructures

  • Two groups, each presenting one case study:
    • Salazar’s ethnographic research on built networks in Antarctica, seeing them as new modes of belonging in the extreme environment
    • Starosielski’s analytical account on the network of undersea cables and its ability of scaling up

Reading: Salazar (2017); Starosielski (2015, Chapter 5). Announcement of the take-home assessment.

Sessions 9+10 (28 November 2019): Study new forms of knowledge

  • In the network society, knowledge production is more situated, partial, and constitutive. How is it possible that we study culture through data?
  • Study cases of people’s negotiation and reaction to contemporary technological transformations
  • Big Data and its discontents

Reading: Barassi (2015, Conclusion); Bunz (2017). Optional: Couldry (2017).

Session 11 (9 January 2020): Study participation

  • The Web’s potential of being more inclusionary and democratic
  • How cultural interpretation, particularly in the networked culture, can become more multifaceted with increased diversity and inclusiveness?

Reading: Gladwell (2010); Kelty (2013).

Session 12 (16 January 2020): Study social media

  • Does our dependency on data reflect a new social order?
  • What is the danger of extracting data from human life (data colonialism) and how social media platforms contribute to this form of capitalisation?
  • Take a closer look at the case of social media activism: the relationship between political activists and social media technologies

Reading: Couldry & Mejias (2019, Chapter 1). Optional: Barassi (2017).

Session 13 (23 January 2020): Oral presentations and discussion

Session 14 (30 January 2020): Doing ethnography & wrap-up session

  • How do networked spaces and global connections influence the way we do ethnography today?
  • Discussing the remaining points
  • Discussing assignments

Reading: Juris & Khasnabish (2013, Introduction). Optional: Tsing (2005, Introduction).

Assessment

  • Take-home assessment (individual), 25–35%. Deadline: 10 pm, Sunday 19 January 2020
  • Participation (individual), 20–30%
  • Presentation (individual), 25–35%
  • Only for 7 credit points: take-home paper (individual), 30%. MAP deadline: 15 March 2020

How to be successful in this course

  • Read all prescribed materials and/or view videos in preparation for each session
  • Attend and engage in all scheduled sessions
  • Start assessment tasks well ahead of the due date, and submit assessments on time
  • Read/listen to all feedback carefully, and consider it for future assessment
  • Engage with fellow students and teaching staff (don’t hesitate to ask questions)
